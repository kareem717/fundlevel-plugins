# LinkedIn Outbound

LinkedIn is FundLevel's introduction channel. Email is the asset-transfer channel. The
goal of any LinkedIn DM is rarely "close the deal in this thread" — it's "get to a state
where the next email isn't cold."

## When to use which channel

| Channel | Best for |
| --- | --- |
| LinkedIn connection request + DM | First touch when you don't have email, or when email has been ignored and you want a different surface. Routing questions. Warm referrals. |
| Email (cold) | First touch when you have a verified address and a strong specific signal. |
| Email (post-LinkedIn warm) | Sending the actual deliverable (shortlist, deck, proposal) after the LinkedIn thread has confirmed interest. |

If a LinkedIn thread is going well, the win condition is *getting the email* — not closing
in DMs. Always ask, even if you already have the email. The act of them sharing it makes
your follow-up a non-cold-email by definition.

## Voice & tone for LinkedIn DMs

Different from email. LinkedIn voice is **more casual** than cold email:

- **Lowercase openers are normal** — "curious;", "oh, awesome!", "amazing".
- **Contractions everywhere** — "doesn't", "I'd", "y'all", "guys'".
- **Single small emojis are fine** — `:)`, `💪`, `🙏`. No emoji clusters, no marketing emojis.
- **Run-on, multi-message rhythm** — break a thought across 2–3 short messages rather than
  one long block. Reads more peer-to-peer.
- **First-name only** — "Hey Margot!", not "Hi Ms. Odell."
- **No signatures** — LinkedIn already shows who you are.

What it should *not* sound like:

- Email-style formal openers ("I hope this finds you well")
- Pitch-deck phrasing ("synergize", "leverage our network", "world-class creators")
- Asking for a 15-minute call in the first three messages
- Any "checking in" / "circling back" energy

## What never to do in a first DM

- **Pitch.** First touch is routing or referral, never a pitch.
- **Ask for a meeting.** Asking for time before you've offered value is a hard pass for most
  recipients on this channel.
- **Send a wall of text.** If your first DM is more than ~4 short bubbles, cut it down.
- **Auto-personalize with surface-level junk.** "Loved your post about [thing]" is fine if
  you actually loved the post and have a specific reason; not fine as a templated opener.

## Talk tracks

  the original prospect (ask for the colleague's email) and the new prospect (warm
  connection request with a name-drop, then run the talk track on them too).

## Universal mechanics across all talk tracks

The patterns below show up in most LinkedIn outbound. When a talk track says "use the
back-pocket offer" or "always bridge to email", these are what it means.

### The proof-of-homework opener

Lead Stage 1 with a **specific** observation about something the prospect's company has
already done. Name a creator, a campaign, a partnership, a hire — something verifiable.

> "I noticed you ran a few collabs earlier this year with guys like Theo"
>
> "I saw some of the work you guys did with folks like bycloud over the last year"

Generic versions don't work:

> "I see you do influencer marketing" — too generic, looks templated.
>
> "I love your brand!" — sycophantic, no information.

### The routing question

Instead of pitching, ask **who handles X**. Even if you suspect the recipient is the right
person — asking this way is lower friction. Either you get routed to the right contact, or
you get a one-word "me!" that opens the door.

> "Curious; do you know who was responsible for handling those partnerships at [company]?"

The question form does two things at once: it surfaces the right contact regardless of who
you've actually messaged, and it gives the recipient an easy reply path (a name, or
"me!"). It deliberately avoids a yes/no commitment ask.

### The back-pocket offer

Once you've confirmed you're talking to the right person, offer a **deliverable**, not a
meeting. Frame it as a free option they can accept without committing to anything.

> "I wanted to forward a shortlist (analytics, URLs, etc.) of a couple creators in our
> network — I think they'd do a great job showcasing [company]. Doesn't hurt to have them
> in your back pocket :)"
>
> "would you be totally against me forwarding a couple profiles (analytics, etc.) your
> way? I think they'd kill it for you guys and I doubt it'd hurt to have em in your back
> pocket :)"

The "back pocket" phrasing is the load-bearing mechanic. It converts a commitment-ask
("want a meeting?") into an optionality-ask ("want this free thing?"). Reply rate on the
optionality-ask is materially higher.

Variations on the same mechanic:

- "doesn't hurt to have them in your back pocket"
- "happy to share if useful — totally up to you"
- "I doubt it'd hurt to have em on hand"
- "would you be against me forwarding…" *(the "totally against" framing makes "yes" feel like the obvious answer)*

### Bridging to email

When they accept the back-pocket offer, get the email — **even if you already have it from
Apollo or CRM**:

> "Amazing, is there an email I can send the google sheet to?"

Why ask for an email you already have: it makes your follow-up email a **warm** email, not
a cold one. The recipient gave you the address; the message is invited; deliverability and
reply rates both go up.

If they say "send it here" (in LinkedIn), send it in LinkedIn — but **then bridge** a
second time: after they review the asset, ask if there's a better email for ongoing
threads. Don't try to run long conversations in DMs.

### Warm connection requests in referral chains

When a contact redirects you to a colleague, send the colleague a connection request with
a **lowercase, casual** name-drop in the note:

> `belle told me about you :)`

Three details matter:

- **All lowercase** — signals casual / peer, not corporate
- **First name only** for the referrer — assumes the referrer is well-known to the
  recipient
- **Single trailing emoji** — `:)` is the right level of warmth; anything more reads
  promotional

Once the connection accepts, the post-connection DM should reference the referrer up
front, then run the rest of the talk track (proof-of-homework + back-pocket offer).

### Always close the loop with the referrer

When someone routes you to a colleague, two things happen in parallel:

1. You message the colleague (connection request + warm DM).
2. You message the **original prospect** to thank them and **ask for the colleague's
   preferred email**:

   > "oh awesome! what would be the best place to contact them? Is that an email I could
   > use?"

This gets you the new prospect's email faster than waiting for LinkedIn to accept, and
makes your eventual email warm (the original prospect implicitly endorsed the channel).

## Anti-patterns to avoid

These break the talk tracks:

- **Pitching in Stage 1.** The whole mechanic depends on Stage 1 being a routing question,
  not a pitch.
- **Skipping the email bridge.** Trying to run the whole sale in DMs limits you to
  LinkedIn's terrible UX for asset sharing and long threads. Get to email.
- **Repeating the back-pocket line verbatim across all DMs.** Vary it — see the
  variations list above.
- **Multi-paragraph DMs.** Break into short bubbles. 1-2 sentences each.
- **Asking for a call before offering the asset.** Asset first, call second.
- **Title-casing or formal punctuation** in casual moments. "Curious - is that still the
  case?" not "Curious. Is that still the case?"
